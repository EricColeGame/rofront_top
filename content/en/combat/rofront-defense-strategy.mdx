RoFront [World PvP] is a real-time territory conquest game on Roblox from creator Torrey Pines. According to the official game page, you expand from a single nation, attack your neighbors, build cities, ports and factories, forge alliances, and launch nukes while competing toward one published win condition: conquer 80% of the world. The description also credits Open Front for the map and states the game is based on Open Front.

That premise matters for defense. RoFront is not a soldier-level shooter, so a RoFront defense strategy is not about cover, rifles, or holding a hallway. It is about borders, neighbors, and the map-wide control meter. This article separates what the official description actually advertises from strategic reasoning that follows from a territory conquest premise, and it flags what is not documented anywhere in the available sources.

## What the Official Description Actually Confirms

Before planning any defense, anchor yourself in verified facts. The official Roblox listing for [RoFront [World PvP]](https://www.roblox.com/games/137151641329864/RoFront) advertises:

- Real-time territory conquest
- Expansion from a single nation
- Attacks on neighbors
- Cities, ports, and factories as buildable options
- Alliances
- Nuclear attacks
- A stated goal of conquering 80% of the world
- A description reference to 88 player nations and hundreds of tribes

Note the wording on that last point. The description mentions 88 player nations. That is a description claim, not a confirmed server capacity. A third-party guide from Nisu observed that Roblox's separate Server Size field showed 30 when checked, and correctly warns against equating the two figures. Treat 88 as an advertised world-scale figure, not a tested simultaneous-player count.

What the sources do not document: specific troop types, damage values, building costs, income formulas, hotkeys, or defense timers. Any guide that invents those numbers for RoFront is guessing.

## Why Defense Deserves Attention in a Conquest Game

Most players instinctively think about offense: where to push, who to attack, how fast to grow. But the published win condition is 80% world control, and the Nisu guide makes a useful observation — a successful local attack improves your position but does not by itself complete the match. That cuts both ways. A lost border region does not end your game either, unless you let it cascade.

The strategic reasoning here is inference from the territory conquest premise, not measured mechanics: in any map-based conquest game, your territory is only as safe as the borders you are not currently watching. Expansion pulls attention outward. Defense is what keeps the map from collapsing behind you.

## Reading Your Borders Before You Commit

The Nisu guide suggests a first-match routine worth repeating: locate your territory, identify its borders, and note which nations share your boundary. The official loop includes attacking neighbors, so knowing exactly who touches you is the starting point for both offense and defense.

A practical defensive habit, inferred from the premise rather than from documented controls:

1. Before any attack, scan every border, not just the one you plan to cross.
2. Identify which neighbor is closest to your core territory.
3. Ask whether the nation you are about to attack has an ally who borders you.
4. Check the world-control display rather than judging progress by how large your colored area looks at the current zoom.

That last point comes directly from the Nisu guide, which notes the world-control meter is a better progress signal than apparent territory size. For defense, the same logic applies: a visually large empire with a thin, exposed frontier is not actually safe.

Nukes, by contrast, are advertised as an attack option, and the Nisu guide reasonably cautions that a dramatic strike in a video does not prove rushing toward it is the best opening. Whether any of these tools can be used defensively is not documented — check the in-game controls before assuming.
