RoFront [World PvP] is a real-time territory conquest game on Roblox by Torrey Pines, built on the Open Front map and played on place 137151641329864. You start with a single nation, expand outward, and compete against other nations and tribes for control of the world. This guide focuses on **RoFront attack strategy**: how to think about when, where, and why you push into a neighbor's territory. ## Start by reading the board before you attack

The single most useful habit in a territory conquest game is to look before you move. Before committing to any push, identify three things:

- **Your borders.** Which nations and tribes actually touch your territory? A neighbor you have not scouted is a neighbor you cannot plan around.
- **The world-control meter.** The official loop is about conquering 80% of the world, so your progress is measured against the whole map, not just your colored area at the current zoom. A big-looking blob can still be far from the goal.
- **Your resources.** The interface shows troop, gold, and attack controls according to third-party match footage. Watch those numbers change as you play so you understand your own tempo.

A third-party guide from Nisu recommends making one deliberate move and observing what changes before repeating it. That is sound advice for attack strategy specifically: your first push teaches you how the game responds, and that response informs your second push.

## Choosing a target: the core of RoFront attack strategy

An attack in RoFront is a territorial commitment. Once you push, you extend your border, and extended borders mean more contact with other nations. That cuts both ways — more room to grow, and more directions you can be pressured from.

Practical reasoning suggests a few questions before any attack:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Does this target share my border? | The official loop is attacking neighbors, so adjacency is the natural starting point. |
| What does the push do to my other borders? | A new conquest can create a new front you did not have before. |
| Am I improving my position toward 80%, or just adding area? | The win condition is world control, not local size. |
| Have I checked who else is nearby? | Overlooking a neighboring threat is one of the failure modes a beginner should investigate after a bad push. |

The Nisu guide frames a failed attack as a diagnostic opportunity: was the target poorly chosen, did you overlook a threat, or did you commit without understanding the controls? Those are the right questions. They are also the reason a single attack percentage or a copied opening from a video cannot be treated as a universal answer.

## Expansion, buildings, and what an attack is actually for

The official description names cities, ports, and factories as things you can build. It does not publish a complete economy table, so no guide can honestly claim a universal cheapest route. What we can say is that these buildings exist as separate options, and the useful first-session question is: **what am I trying to improve with this choice?**

From a territory conquest premise, an attack strategy and a building strategy are connected. Territory is how you grow; buildings are how you develop what you hold. If you are constantly attacking without developing, you may be spreading thin. If you are constantly building without expanding, you may be ceding the map to neighbors who are not standing still. The balance is yours to find in your own matches, because the listing does not establish an optimal order.

## A simple first-match attack plan

If you want a starting routine rather than a build order, try this:

1. Open [RoFront [World PvP]](https://www.roblox.com/games/137151641329864/RoFront) and locate your territory and its borders.
2. Find the world-control display and note where you stand against the 80% goal.
3. Identify one adjacent nation or tribe.
4. Make one deliberate attack and watch what changes — territory, resources, and any shift along another border.
5. Inspect one building option and read its displayed cost and benefit before buying.
6. After the match, review the move that changed your position most.

This is a learning loop, not a winning formula. The Nisu guide, a third-party source, offers a similar first-run approach and notes it has not played a live match itself — a useful reminder to treat all outside advice as secondary to what you observe in the actual experience.
