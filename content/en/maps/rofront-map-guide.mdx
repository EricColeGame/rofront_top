The map is the game in RoFront [World PvP]. Torrey Pines built a real-time territory conquest experience on Roblox where you start as a single nation on a shared world map, expand outward, and race other players toward control of the globe. This RoFront map guide focuses on what the map actually tells you, how to read it before you commit to a move, and where the documented information ends and your own observation has to begin.

Everything below is grounded in the official Roblox listing for [RoFront [World PvP]](https://www.roblox.com/games/137151641329864/RoFront) and, where noted, a third-party guide from Nisu. ## What the RoFront map actually is

RoFront is not a soldier-level shooter. There are no classes, loadouts, or kill counts to chase. The "map" here is a political and territorial board: colored nations packed against each other, borders that shift as players expand, and a world-control meter that tracks how close someone is to the finish line.

The official description frames the loop clearly: expand from a single nation, attack your neighbors, build cities, ports, and factories, forge alliances, and launch nukes. The stated victory condition is conquering 80% of the world. That number is the anchor for every map decision you make, because a big-looking blob of color at your current zoom level is not the same thing as measurable world control.

One important caveat on scale: the description mentions 88 player nations and hundreds of tribes. That is a description of the game world, not a confirmed server capacity. Roblox lists server size as a separate field, and the two figures should not be treated as the same thing. Check the current server information on the game page before you join if player count matters to you.

## Reading your starting position

Your first useful act in a match is not attacking. It is locating yourself.

Find your territory, trace its borders, and identify which nations share those borders. The official loop includes attacking neighbors, so knowing who your neighbors are is the prerequisite for any expansion plan. A border you never looked at is a border you cannot defend or exploit.

Then find the world-control display. The Nisu guide, an independent third-party resource, makes a pointed observation here: judging your progress by how large your colored area looks at the current zoom is unreliable. The control meter is the honest number. Zoom changes perception; the meter does not.

A reasonable first-match routine, offered as strategy rather than measured mechanics:

1. Identify your nation and its full border.
2. Note every adjacent nation by name or color.
3. Locate the world-control meter and read your current value.
4. Make one deliberate move and watch what changes.
5. Check whether any other border shifted while you were busy.

That last step matters more than most beginners expect. In a real-time conquest game with many nations on one map, the world does not pause while you push in one direction.

## Borders, neighbors, and the cost of a bad push

Territory conquest logic suggests a few things worth testing in your own matches. These are inferences from the genre premise, not documented mechanics:

- A neighbor with a long shared border is a bigger ongoing concern than one you barely touch.
- Expanding in one direction usually thins your presence somewhere else.
- The nation that looks weakest may be weak because someone else is already pressuring it, which cuts both ways.

When a push goes badly, the useful question is not "what percentage should I have attacked with" but "which decision would I change?" Was the target poorly chosen? Did you ignore a second border? Did you commit before you understood the current controls? Those questions are answerable from your own match, and they compound into real map sense.

## Buildings, alliances, and nukes on the map

The official description names cities, ports, and factories as buildable, plus alliances and nuclear attacks as features. It does not publish costs, build times, or an economy table, and no third-party source reviewed here establishes a universal optimal build order. Treat any claim of a "cheapest route to victory" with suspicion.

What you can do is inspect each option in-game and ask what you are trying to improve. A port and a factory presumably serve different purposes, but the listing does not define them, so read the in-game description and displayed requirements before spending.

| Advertised feature | What to check in your match |
|---|---|
| Territory expansion | Your borders, your target, world-control progress |
| Cities, ports, factories | Current description, cost, intended benefit |
| Alliances | Who is involved, what the controls offer |
| Nuclear attacks | Requirements and consequences of the action |

Alliances and nukes are the two features most likely to change your map position dramatically, and both are relationship-altering. Read the relevant controls before acting. A dramatic strike in someone's video does not mean rushing toward it is right for your position.
