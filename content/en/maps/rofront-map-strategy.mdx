RoFront [World PvP] is a real-time territory conquest game on Roblox from creator Torrey Pines. According to the official game page, you expand from a single nation, attack your neighbors, build cities, ports, and factories, forge alliances, and launch nukes while competing against other nations and tribes. The stated victory condition is to conquer 80% of the world. The description also credits Open Front for the map and says the game is based on Open Front.

That matters for map strategy: RoFront is not a soldier-level shooter. There are no classes, rifles, or kill/death ratios to manage. The map itself is the board, and your decisions are about where your colored territory sits, who touches your borders, and how close the world-control meter is to the advertised 80% threshold. Everything below is strategic reasoning drawn from that territory-conquest premise, not measured game mechanics.

## Start by reading the map, not by attacking

The most useful first move in any RoFront match is informational. Locate your nation, trace its borders, and identify every neighbor that shares a boundary with you. The official description confirms that attacking neighbors is part of the loop, so knowing exactly who borders you is the foundation of any map plan.

Then find the world-control display. A third-party guide from Nisu, which describes itself as an independent, unplayed observation of the interface, recommends watching the world-control meter rather than judging progress by how large your colored area looks at the current zoom level. That is sound advice for a map game: zoomed-in territory can look impressive while the actual control percentage barely moves.

A simple first-session routine: make one deliberate expansion, then pause and observe what changed. Did your border shift? Did another nation's border move? Did the control meter tick? That single observation gives you a reference point for the next decision without relying on an unverified opening build order.

## Treat borders as your real strategic map

In a territory conquest game, borders are the only thing that matters tactically. Your map strategy should answer three questions before every push:

| Map question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Who borders the territory I want? | A new border means a new neighbor who can pressure you |
| Does expanding here thin my other borders? | Overextension is a map problem, not a combat problem |
| Is this push moving the world-control meter? | The advertised win condition is 80% world control |

The official description does not publish troop counts, attack formulas, or expansion costs, so no guide can honestly tell you "attack at X percent." What the description does establish is the shape of the game: neighbors, territory, and a global control target. Plan around that shape.

## Cities, ports, and factories: learn them as separate map tools

The official description names cities, ports, and factories as buildable structures. It does not publish a complete economy table, costs, or a universal optimal build order. Any guide claiming a single cheapest route is inventing numbers.

What you can do is treat each building as a map decision with a purpose. Before building, ask what you are trying to improve: holding a border, supporting a coastal position, or strengthening an interior region. Then read the in-game description and cost shown for that structure, compare it to your current situation, and watch the result. The Nisu guide makes the same point: inspect the current options and their displayed requirements rather than copying whichever icon appears first in a video.

Ports are especially interesting from a map perspective because they imply coastal or sea-adjacent positioning. If your nation touches water, a port may matter more to your map plan than it would to a landlocked interior start. That is inference from the feature name and the territory premise, not a documented mechanic.

## Alliances and nukes change the map politically

The official description lists alliances and nuclear attacks as features. Both are relationship-changing actions on the map. A nuke is not just damage; it redraws borders and reshapes who your neighbors are. An alliance is not just safety; it can free you to push in one direction while a partner covers another.

Because the description does not document requirements, targeting rules, or consequences, verify these systems inside the actual experience before committing. Read the relevant in-game controls, especially for any action that changes your standing with other nations. A dramatic nuclear strike in a video does not prove that rushing toward nukes is the right opening for your position.

## The 88-nation claim and server size are different things

The official description mentions 88 player nations and hundreds of tribes. That is an advertised feature of the game world. It is not the same as Roblox's separate Server Size field, which the Nisu guide observed showing 30 when checked. The listing does not explain how those two figures relate, so do not assume an 88-player server. Check the current Roblox server information when you join.

## A practical map strategy checklist

- Identify your nation, borders, and every adjacent neighbor before your first attack.
- Watch the world-control meter, not just your colored area at the current zoom.
- Make one move, observe the result, then decide again.
- Inspect each city, port, and factory option and its displayed cost before building.
- Read alliance and nuclear controls before using them, since both change the political map.
- Keep the advertised 80% conquest goal in view; a strong local position is not a win.
