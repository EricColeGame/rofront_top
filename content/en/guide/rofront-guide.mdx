RoFront [World PvP] is a real-time territory conquest game on Roblox from the creator Torrey Pines. According to its official Roblox page, it is based on the Open Front game, with map credits to Open Front, and it asks you to expand from a single nation, attack your neighbors, build cities, ports, and factories, forge alliances, and launch nukes. The advertised victory condition is conquering 80% of the world.

That is a very different experience from a shooter. There are no classes, rifles, or kill/death ratios to track here. This RoFront guide focuses on what the official description actually supports: reading the map, expanding deliberately, and treating buildings and diplomacy as decisions rather than decoration. ## What RoFront Actually Is

The official description frames RoFront as real-time territory conquest. You begin with one nation, share borders with other nations, and compete for control of a world map. The description also mentions 88 player nations and hundreds of tribes. Treat that as a description of the game's scope, not a promise that any single server holds 88 live players. Roblox lists server capacity separately, and the two numbers should not be conflated.

Because RoFront is map-based and strategic, tutorials for similarly named Roblox shooters will not transfer. If you search for the game, use the official listing and confirm the creator is Torrey Pines before joining. Similar names can lead to unrelated experiences.

## Reading the Map Before You Attack

The most useful first habit is observation. Before you commit to any attack, locate your own territory, identify which nations share your borders, and find the world-control display. That meter matters more than how large your colored area looks at your current zoom level, because the win condition is measured in world control, not local size.

A practical first-session routine, offered here as strategy inferred from a territory conquest premise rather than a measured mechanic:

1. Find your nation and its borders.
2. Note your neighbors and whether any border looks exposed.
3. Check the world-control meter to see where you actually stand.
4. Make one deliberate move, then watch what changes before repeating it.

If a push goes poorly, ask which decision you would change: the target, the timing, or an overlooked neighbor. Those questions are more useful than copying a build order from a video, because the official listing does not publish an optimal opening.

A reasonable approach is to ask what you are trying to improve with each choice. A port and a factory may serve different purposes, but the listing does not establish which is cheaper or better in a given position. Inspect the option, compare it to your situation, and watch the result.

Alliances and nukes deserve extra caution because both can change relationships with other nations. Read the relevant in-game controls before acting. A dramatic nuclear strike in a video does not prove that rushing toward nukes is the right opening for your position.

Third-party footage can help you learn the interface. The better use of footage is to pause on the interface, match one visible control to the action that follows, and then watch the result. Do not copy a number from a single frame. Resources, neighbors, and territory change throughout a match, so a snapshot is not a strategy.
