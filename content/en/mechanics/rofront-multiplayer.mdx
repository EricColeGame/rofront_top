RoFront multiplayer is not a shooter with squads and rifles. It is a map-based, real-time territory conquest game on Roblox, published by Torrey Pines under the name RoFront [World PvP]. The official description states that it is based on the Open Front game, credits Open Front for the map, and frames the whole experience around expanding from a single nation, attacking neighbors, and competing for control of the world. If you searched for RoFront multiplayer expecting classes, loadouts, or kill counts, that is a different genre entirely. This article sticks to what the official listing actually advertises and separates that from strategy advice and from details that simply are not documented yet.

## What the official description establishes

The verified Roblox description is short but specific. It advertises:

- Real-time territory conquest
- Starting from a single nation
- Attacking your neighbors
- Building cities, ports, and factories
- Forging alliances
- Launching nukes
- Competing against "88 player nations and hundreds of tribes"
- Winning by conquering 80% of the world

Those are the advertised features. Everything else — exact costs, income rates, unit types, damage values, hotkeys, or build timings — is not in the official description, and this article will not invent it. The description also credits Open Front for the map, which tells you the geography is borrowed, but it does not mean Open Front's own rules or balance numbers apply to RoFront.

One important nuance: the phrase "88 player nations" comes from the description. It is not a confirmed server capacity. A third-party guide from Nisu noted that Roblox's separate Server Size field showed 30 when checked, and correctly pointed out that the listing does not explain how the two figures relate. Treat 88 as an advertised world-scale figure, not a promise about how many humans share your server.

## Why "multiplayer" here means shared map pressure

In a territory conquest game, multiplayer changes the meaning of every decision. You are not queueing into a match to shoot opponents; you are sharing a persistent-feeling world map where other nations expand, border you, and react. The practical consequence is that your borders are the most important information on screen. The Nisu guide recommends locating your territory, its borders, and your neighboring nations before committing to anything, and watching the world-control display rather than judging progress by how large your colored area looks at the current zoom.

That is sound advice inferred from the genre, not a measured mechanic. In any conquest game, an attack that looks profitable can expose a different border. The official description confirms attacking neighbors is part of the loop, so knowing who shares your boundary is a reasonable first step.

## Expansion, buildings, and alliances as advertised systems

The description names cities, ports, and factories as things you can build, and alliances and nukes as things you can do. It does not provide an economy table, so no one can honestly claim a universal "best first build." A useful habit is to inspect each option's displayed information and cost in your own match, then ask what you are trying to improve before buying. The Nisu guide makes the same point: treat cities, ports, and factories as separate options to learn rather than copying whichever icon a video shows first.

| Advertised feature | What to verify in your match |
|---|---|
| Territory expansion | Your borders, your target, world-control progress |
| Cities, ports, factories | Current description, cost, intended benefit |
| Alliances | Who is involved, what the controls offer |
| Nuclear attacks | Requirements and consequences of the action |

Alliances deserve caution. The description confirms they exist, but it does not explain their terms. Before acting on anything that changes relationships with other nations, read the in-game controls. A dramatic nuclear strike in a video does not prove that rushing toward nukes is the right opening for your position.

## The 80% goal and how to think about progress

The published victory condition is conquering 80% of the world. That is a match-level goal, not a per-attack target. A successful local push can improve your position without ending the game, so the world-control meter matters more than the size of your territory at one zoom level. Strategy advice here is inference from a conquest premise: make one deliberate move, watch what changes, and use that as your reference for the next decision. If a push goes poorly, ask whether the target was poorly chosen, whether you missed a neighboring threat, or whether you acted without understanding the current controls.

## FAQ

**How do you win RoFront multiplayer?**
The official description says to conquer 80% of the world. Follow the world-control display rather than assuming one big territory has won.

**Does RoFront support 88 players in one server?**
The description mentions 88 player nations, but that is an advertised figure, not a tested capacity. Check Roblox's current server information when joining.

**What should I build first?**
Inspect the current city, port, and factory options and choose based on your position. No universal optimal order is documented.

**Is RoFront a shooter?**
No. It is real-time territory conquest on a map, not a soldier first-person shooter.

For a first session, keep it simple: find your nation, note your neighbors, make one measured expansion, inspect one building, and watch the control meter move toward the stated 80% goal. That is the loop the official listing actually describes.
