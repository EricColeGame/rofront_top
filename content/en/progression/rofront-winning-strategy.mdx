RoFront [World PvP] is a real-time territory conquest game on Roblox from creator Torrey Pines. According to the official game page, it is based on the Open Front game, with map credits to Open Front. You begin with a single nation, expand outward, attack neighbors, build cities, ports, and factories, forge alliances, and launch nukes while competing against other nations and tribes. The advertised victory condition is to conquer 80% of the world.

That single sentence — conquer 80% of the world — is the spine of any RoFront winning strategy. Everything else is a means of moving the world-control meter toward that number without losing what you already hold. This guide separates what the official description actually states from strategic reasoning that follows from a territory conquest premise, and it flags what is not documented so you can verify it yourself in the live experience.

## Start by reading the board, not by attacking

The most common beginner mistake in any territory conquest game is expanding before understanding the map. The official description confirms you start as one nation among many, so your first useful action is reconnaissance, not aggression.

Open [RoFront [World PvP]](https://www.roblox.com/games/137151641329864/RoFront) and identify three things before you commit to anything:

- Where your territory sits and which nations or tribes share your borders.
- Where the world-control display is and what it currently reads.
- What your nation's current resources and options look like in the interface.

A third-party guide from Nisu, which describes itself as an independent, unplayed observation of the interface, recommends making one deliberate move and watching what changes before repeating it. That is sound advice regardless of the specific mechanics: it builds your own reference data instead of relying on someone else's opening build order. The Nisu guide also notes that a large colored area at your current zoom level is not the same as progress on the world-control meter, so judge yourself by the meter, not by how impressive your blob looks.

## Expansion is a means, not the goal

The official description lists attacking your neighbors as part of the core loop. From a territory conquest premise, the strategic logic is straightforward: every territory you take adds to your share of world control, but every border you create is also a border you must eventually defend.

Practical reasoning suggests a few habits:

- Prefer targets that are weakly connected to strong neighbors, so a single push does not open you to three new fronts.
- Consolidate before you sprint. A thin, sprawling territory is harder to hold than a compact one.
- Watch other nations' behavior while you expand. In a live PvP match, the players around you are also reading the board.

None of this is a measured mechanic. The official listing does not publish combat formulas, attack costs, or expansion rates, so treat these as inference from the genre, not as verified numbers.

The official description names cities, ports, and factories as buildable structures. It does not publish a cost table, an income formula, or a recommended build order. That absence matters: anyone claiming a universal "best first building" is going beyond what the developer has documented.

A useful way to approach each build is to ask what problem it solves for your current position. The Nisu guide frames this well by suggesting you inspect the displayed description and cost of each option and compare it to your situation rather than copying whatever a video shows first.

## Alliances and nukes are relationship decisions

Alliances and nuclear attacks appear in the official description as advertised features. Both are the kind of action that changes how other nations treat you, so the strategic suggestion here is caution rather than enthusiasm.

From a territory conquest premise, an alliance is most valuable when it secures a border you would otherwise have to defend, freeing resources for expansion elsewhere. A nuke, by contrast, is a dramatic action that a video might showcase without establishing that rushing toward it is a good opening. The Nisu guide makes this point directly: a dramatic nuclear strike in a clip does not prove it is the right first move for your position.

Read the relevant in-game controls before acting. The official listing does not document the requirements or consequences of either system, so the live interface is your authority.

## The 88-nation claim and server reality

The official description says you compete against 88 player nations and hundreds of tribes. That is a description of the game's scope, not a verified server capacity. The Nisu guide notes that Roblox's separate Server Size field showed 30 when checked, and that the listing does not explain how the two figures relate. Treat 88 as an advertised figure and check the current Roblox server information when you join.
