RoFront [World PvP] is a real-time territory conquest game on Roblox from the creator Torrey Pines. According to the official game page, it is based on the Open Front game, with map credits to Open Front. You begin with a single nation, expand outward, attack neighbors, build cities, ports, and factories, forge alliances, and launch nukes while competing against many other nations and tribes. The advertised victory condition is to conquer 80% of the world.

That premise makes RoFront country strategy a progression question rather than a combat question. You are not managing a soldier, a loadout, or a firefight. You are managing a country: its borders, its economy, its relationships, and its share of the map. This guide separates what the official description actually advertises from strategic reasoning you can apply in a match, and it flags what the sources do not document.

## What the official description establishes

The verified description on the [official RoFront page](https://www.roblox.com/games/137151641329864/RoFront) is the authority here. It confirms:

- Real-time territory conquest
- Starting from a single nation
- Attacking neighboring nations
- Building cities, ports, and factories
- Forging alliances
- Launching nukes
- Competing against "88 player nations and hundreds of tribes"
- Winning by conquering 80% of the world

Two cautions matter. First, the "88 player nations" figure comes from the description. It is not the same as server capacity. A third-party guide from Nisu notes that Roblox's separate Server Size field showed 30 when checked, and that the listing does not explain how the two numbers relate. Treat 88 as an advertised world scale, not a tested simultaneous-player count.

Second, the description names features but does not publish costs, income formulas, build timings, or balance values. Any specific number you see in a video may reflect that player's situation, not a universal rule.

## Reading your position before you expand

Territory conquest games reward information before action. The Nisu guide, a third-party resource, suggests a sensible first-match routine: locate your territory, identify your borders, and note which nations share them. It also recommends watching the world-control display rather than judging progress by how large your colored area looks at the current zoom.

That advice follows directly from the premise. If victory requires 80% of the world, then your local territory size is only one input. The world-control meter tells you how far the whole match has moved. A large-looking blob at one zoom level can still be a small fraction of the map.

A practical opening approach, inferred from the conquest premise rather than measured mechanics:

1. Identify your immediate neighbors before committing anywhere.
2. Make one deliberate move and observe what changes.
3. Check whether another border shifted while you were occupied.
4. Note what information the interface shows for your nation after the move.

This gives you a reference point for your next decision without relying on an unverified build order.

## Cities, ports, and factories as separate decisions

The official description lists cities, ports, and factories as buildable. It does not say which is best first, and it does not provide an economy table. The Nisu guide makes the same point: inspect the current options and their displayed costs before deciding.

A useful question for each building is: what am I trying to improve right now? Because the description does not define each building's effect, you should read the in-game information shown for that building and compare it with your present situation. Treat the three as separate systems to learn rather than assuming one icon is always correct.

| Advertised feature | What to check in your match |
|---|---|
| Territory expansion | Your borders, your target, world-control progress |
| Cities, ports, factories | Displayed description, cost, and intended benefit |
| Alliances | Who is involved and what the current controls offer |
| Nuclear attacks | Requirements and consequences of the selected action |

## Alliances and nukes change relationships

Alliances and nuclear attacks are both advertised. Both are relationship-changing actions, which is why the Nisu guide recommends reading the relevant in-game controls before acting. A dramatic nuclear strike in a video does not establish that rushing toward nukes is the right opening for your position.

From a strategy standpoint, alliances in a many-nation conquest game usually serve to reduce the number of fronts you are fighting on. That is reasoning from the premise, not a documented mechanic. The description does not explain how alliances are formed, broken, or enforced, so verify through the actual experience.

## The 80% goal and how to think about progress

The published win condition is conquering 80% of the world. A successful local attack can improve your position without completing the match. Keep the world-control display in view and treat each expansion as a step toward that threshold rather than a finish line.

Player footage can help you learn the interface. The Nisu guide references an APEX ROBLOX video from October 10 that follows a match from a small territory to a larger one, showing the world-control goal, colored borders, and troop, gold, and attack controls. Note that the sampled segment carries a 2× speed marker, so do not read its playback timing as real-time income or attack speed.

## FAQ

**How do you win RoFront?**
The official description says to conquer 80% of the world. Follow the world-control display rather than assuming one large territory has won.

**Does RoFront support 88 players in one server?**
The description mentions 88 player nations. Roblox's separate Server Size field showed 30 when the Nisu guide checked. These are different fields, and live capacity has not been verified here.

**What should I build first?**
Inspect the current city, port, and factory options and choose based on your position and their displayed requirements. No universal optimal order is documented.

**Is RoFront a shooter?**
No. It is a real-time territory conquest game about nations, borders, and world control, not a first-person shooter.
